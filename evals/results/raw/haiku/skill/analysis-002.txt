## Verdict: INVALID

The proof does not establish the claim, and the claim is false as stated.

## Claim and assumptions

- **Claim:** For a pointwise convergent sequence $f_n \to f$ on $[0,1]$, $\lim_n \int_0^1 f_n = \int_0^1 f$.
- **Assumptions:** Only pointwise convergence. The proof does not assume integrability, measurability, a uniform bound, or domination.

## First unsupported step

**Step 2:** "Integrate both sides of the pointwise limit."

Pointwise convergence gives $\lim_n f_n(x) = f(x)$ for each $x$. Integrating both sides, which is what the proof does, gives $\int \lim_n f_n = \int f$. It does not give $\lim_n \int f_n = \int f$. Passing the limit through the integral is exactly the exchange the claim asserts, so step 2 assumes the conclusion. Step 1 only sets up the hypotheses, so it is not the first failure.

## Why it fails

Exchanging limit and integral requires an additional hypothesis such as:
- uniform convergence on a finite-measure space,
- a dominating integrable function $g$ with $|f_n| \le g$ (dominated convergence), or
- monotonicity with nonnegative functions (monotone convergence).

Pointwise convergence alone is not enough, because mass can escape to a shrinking set where the functions are large.

## Counterexample

Let $f_n = n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$.

- **Pointwise limit:** For $x > 0$, $1/n < x$ for all large $n$, so $f_n(x) = 0$ eventually. Also $f_n(0) = 0$. Hence $f_n \to f = 0$ pointwise.
- **Integrals:** $\int_0^1 f_n = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$ for every $n$, while $\int_0^1 f = 0$.

So $\lim_n \int f_n = 1 \ne 0 = \int f$. Every function here is Lebesgue measurable and integrable, so the failure is not caused by a missing integrability hypothesis.
