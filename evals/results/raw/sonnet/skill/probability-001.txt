**Claim and assumptions.** X and Y are random variables, presumably with finite second moments so that Cov is defined. The target is that Cov(X,Y)=0 implies X and Y are independent. No further hypotheses are given, such as joint Gaussianity.

**Counterexample.** Let X be uniform on {-1, 0, 1} and Y = X².
- E[X] = 0 and E[XY] = E[X³] = 0, so Cov(X,Y) = 0.
- P(X=0, Y=0) = 1/3, but P(X=0)·P(Y=0) = (1/3)(1/3) = 1/9.
- So X and Y are not independent, and the claim is false.

**First unsupported step: step 3.**
- Step 1 is just the assumption.
- Step 2 is a correct restatement of Cov(X,Y)=0, since Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] when the moments are finite.
- Step 3 does not follow. Independence needs E[f(X)g(Y)] = E[f(X)]E[g(Y)] for all bounded measurable f and g (equivalently, the joint law equals the product of the marginals). Step 2 gives this only for f = g = identity. In the counterexample, f(x)=x² and g(y)=y already fail. Zero covariance only rules out linear dependence.

**Repair (labeled as such).** The statement becomes true if (X,Y) is jointly Gaussian. It also holds in the trivial case where X and Y are each two-valued (indicator-type), since then the linear test functions span all functions of each variable. Without such an added hypothesis, the claim cannot be proved.
